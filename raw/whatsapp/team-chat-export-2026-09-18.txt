[8/9/26, 7:25:35 AM] Jason Hu: **Your understanding is highly accurate.** The BIOGLOW™️ Future Edition represents a significant paradigm shift from the traditional (legacy) FIRST LEGO League Challenge. While legacy FLL heavily emphasizes complex autonomous programming and highly intricate mobile robot navigation, the Future Edition shifts its focus toward **real-time sports-like coordination, speed, and collaborative execution**.

Here is a breakdown of why your understanding is spot-on, grounded in the official rules and roles:

### 1. **Much More Coordination and Communication**
In traditional FLL matches, only two technicians stand at the base to launch and swap attachments on a single autonomous robot. In the BIOGLOW Future Edition, coordination is the defining element of the game:
* **Four Active Roles Simultaneously:** Each match requires four players on the field, each assigned to a highly specialized, active role: **Driver, Operator, Technician, and Specialist**. 
* **Strict Spatial Boundaries:** Players must stay completely inside their designated physical areas and are not allowed to switch roles during the match. 
* **No Passing by Hand:** Because players cannot leave their areas, nothing can be pushed, thrown, or passed by hand between teammates. If the Driver needs a token from the Technician Area, or the Specialist needs resources to recycle, the team must physically coordinate and transfer items **solely using their tools**. This structure demands constant verbal communication and precise timing to synchronize movements.

### 2. **Increased Focus on Speed and Execution**
Because the 2:30 match is played in real-time with multiple players actively operating on the field at once, raw execution speed and manual dexterity play a much larger role:
* The **Operator** must manually load, aim, and physically launch resource tokens into the grand tree base using a mechanical launcher.
* The **Driver** is actively navigating the terrain in real-time, meaning driver skill and quick maneuvering directly dictate the pace of the match.

### 3. **Significantly Less Programming Required**
In legacy FLL, the robot must run 100% autonomously, meaning success is entirely dependent on writing complex code (e.g., sensor calibration, PID line followers, gyro navigation). In the BIOGLOW Future Edition, **autonomous programming is largely optional or simplified**:
* **Manual Remote Control is Allowed:** Up to two handheld controllers are permitted (one for the Driver and one for the Specialist). The Driver's base can run autonomously, but it is primarily designed to be manually operated by the controller to collect and transport resources.
* **Purely Mechanical Tools:** The **Operator's tool** is purely mechanical, constructed entirely out of non-electric LEGO bricks, requiring **zero code or electronics** to function.
* **Simplified Sensor Control:** The **Technician's tool** only utilizes a single motor and a color sensor. While it can be programmed to run autonomously, the rules also permit it to be controlled directly through basic motor/sensor inputs (such as triggering a motor when a color is detected). 
* *Note:* Coding is still a part of the experience (using the LEGO Education Coding Canvas to program optional autonomous routines or modify tool functions), but it is no longer the sole gatekeeper to completing missions.

### 4. **Streamlined and Specialized Robotic Design**
Instead of engineering a single, highly complex robot that must carry massive modular attachments to autonomously navigate the entire board, the robotic design in BIOGLOW is decentralized:
* The engineering is divided into **simpler, specialized tools** tailored to each role. For example, the Driver builds a basic dual-motor driving base, the Operator builds a non-electric mechanical launcher, and the Specialist operates the dual motor pre-embedded inside the stationary Grand Tree model. 

### **Summary**
Your intuition is correct. The legacy version is an **autonomous engineering and software challenge**, while the future edition is a **real-time collaborative team sport** where physical execution, division of labor, driver control, and active communication are the keys to victory.

We reviewed the official promotional announcements introducing the new Computer Science & AI (Future Edition) platform.
We completed our official team registration on the FIRST Dashboard under the Future Edition track without restriction.

To be entirely candid: The most frustrating part is not that Southern California may face logistical constraints in offering the Future Edition. The most frustrating part is that families and teams were encouraged and permitted to invest significant time and financial resources before being clearly informed that this possibility existed in their region.

Over the past two days, we have spoken with several veteran FLL coaches and longtime volunteers in our local community. The reaction has been remarkably consistent across the board: complete surprise and confusion. New teams inherently trust that official national registration pathways lead to viable local participation. Families should not have to discover through private correspondence that their chosen pathway does not exist in their region after making substantial non-refundable commitments.

Furthermore, we observe that other regions, including Northern California (NorCal) and Texas, have successfully published competitive pathways accommodating both Founders and Future divisions. Southern California is one of the nation's premier technology and STEM hubs; having newly registered teams completely disenfranchised from a competitive season feels like a missed opportunity for our students.

Most importantly, our young students—who are 9 years old—are brimming with excitement to explore AI, coding, and robotics. They believe they are building the future of FLL.  It is heartbreaking to consider telling these dedicated children that despite following all instructions and working diligently, they might not have a real tournament stage to present their Innovation Project and demonstrate their robot. 

Because we care deeply about FIRST and wish to find a constructive, positive way forward, we respectfully request your guidance and consideration on the following:

Exploring an Official Future Division in SoCal: We urge Southern California leadership to consider piloting an official, competitive Future Edition qualifying division (even on a modest scale) during the regular season, allowing teams to experience full project judging and robot performance.
Coordinated Cross-Regional Eligibility: If Southern California cannot host a competitive Future track this year, we request formal coordination with Northern California or adjacent regions to guarantee registration eligibility for SoCal Future Edition teams to compete in their official qualifying events without administrative barriers.
Transparent Public Guidance: A clear and prompt public announcement outlining Southern California's roadmap for 2026–2027 so that all affected teams and families can make timely, informed decisions for their students.
We write this letter not out of contention, but out of a shared passion for STEM education and a commitment to advocacy for our students. Also, we believe there are many new families and teams still don’t know what happened and eager to know what’s going on.  We deeply appreciate the tireless efforts of our regional partners and volunteers who make FIRST possible, and we hope to work collaboratively toward a solution that allows these eager young minds to compete and shine this season.

Thank you very much for your time, leadership, and prompt attention to this matter.

Thank you for reaching out and for explaining your project. This sounds like a wonderful way for the students to learn about beneficial insects and observe the green lacewing life cycle firsthand.

For a project involving four families, we would actually recommend starting with a much smaller quantity than the standard 1,000-egg card and 400-larvae unit. Those quantities are intended more for biological control applications and would be considerably more than you need for an educational observation project.

For your project, we would suggest approximately 50–100 green lacewing eggs total.

This would allow you to divide the eggs among the four families, giving each family a small number to observe. With proper care, the students can potentially observe the entire life cycle starting with the egg rather than starting only with larvae.

Lacewing Life Cycle
Egg → Larva → Cocoon/Pupa → Adult

We can add a larval unit, but the cells are small and you really wouldn't be able to observe the life stages unless you pulled off the paper or the organza that covers the cells.

One important consideration is that lacewing larvae are predators and will cannibalize one another, particularly when kept together. We therefore recommend keeping the larvae separated by providing individual/small rearing spaces once they hatch, otherwise you will wind up with one big larvae. This also makes it easier for the students to follow the development of individual insects.

Care and Feeding the larvae

Because you are raising a relatively small number, you should only need a small quantity of frozen ephestia eggs. 

For an educational project, we would recommend keeping the numbers small and setting up simple individual observation containers so the students can easily see what is happening at each stage.

We make our larval units the Thursday before shipping the following week. We receive our fresh eggs on Tuesdays. This would be an ideal time to ship. San Diego is in a one day transit time from us here in Ventura, so we would be able to ship via UPS ground service for about $10 to $17.
The next available ship date would be 9/15.

Thank you again for including green lacewings in your project. We would be happy to help you put together a small educational setup that gives the students the best opportunity to observe the life cycle.

TIPS

Lacewing larvae have long, curved, sickle-shaped jaws that lock together to pierce prey and suck out body fluids.

Structure of the Mouthparts (see attached)
Mandibles and Maxillae: Each side features a pair of elongated mouthparts—an outer mandible (the curved, sharp grasping jaw) and an inner maxilla (which fits neatly inside a groove in the mandible to form a functional straw-like tube).

Hollow Channels: Tiny internal grooves create hollow food channels running between the interlocking jaws.

How They Feed
Injecting Enzymes: The larva stabs its prey (such as an aphid) and injects paralyzing and digestive enzymes straight into the victim.

Liquifying Prey: The internal organs of the prey dissolve into a liquid broth within seconds

Sucking Fluids: The larva vacuums up the digested nutrients through the hollow channels in its jaws


Lacewing larva bites are devastatingly lethal to insect prey, but they only cause mild, temporary irritation to humans. Because these larvae lack venom glands, their bite on human skin is usually just an accidental pinch or a minor reaction to their digestive saliva.

The Bite Sensation: Feels like a sudden, sharp needle prick.

Skin Reaction: A small red welt or bump typically develops.

Duration: Itching and swelling usually resolve within a few days.

Basic Treatment: Wash the area with soap and water. Use a cold compress to reduce swelling.

To safely handle lacewing larvae without getting nipped, the golden rule is to avoid direct skin contact whenever possible. Because they are voracious hunters, they may bite human skin purely out of curiosity or searching for moisture.

Safe Moving Techniques
Use a soft paintbrush: Gently sweep the larva onto the bristles of a soft makeup or paint brush to relocate it.

Coax onto a leaf: Place a leaf, piece of paper, or a twig directly in front of the larva and let it crawl onto the surface.

Wear protective gloves: Wear lightweight garden or thin gardening gloves if you must touch plants heavily populated with them.

What to Avoid
Do not pick them up with bare fingers: Their tiny jaws easily pinch bare skin.

Do not squeeze them: They are fragile and easily crushed if handled with too much force.

Keep away from bare arms: Avoid brushing bare arms or neck against heavy foliage where larvae are actively hunting.
