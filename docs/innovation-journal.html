First farm visit — Coastal Roots Farm

Morning at the farm, 9:00–12:00, before the evening robot session. What the kids learned:

  • Regenerative organic farming — the chicken-and-plant circle, how animals and crops feed each other
  • Invasive species: Bermuda grass, spread by underground runners; and a rabbit problem in the crops
  • Healthy soil — what makes soil alive rather than just dirt
  • Questions put to an expert — the tour guide — then a brainstorm of problems and ideas to help the farm

Two real, named problems came out of the day. Both were about controlling something unwanted on a farm that cannot spray.

The project is chosen: lacewings

The kids studied the insect ecosystem — ladybugs, lacewings and hoverflies, the predators that keep pests down without spraying — and chose to build the project around the lacewing.

It looks like a shift away from Bermuda grass and rabbits, but it is the same question answered another way: biological pest control on a farm that cannot spray.

Needs a source. The session recorded that a lacewing eats thousands of aphids a day. Published figures are far lower, so the real number has to be found and cited before a judge hears it.

Second farm tour — soil, wasps and a four-rung ladder

Beneficial insects and pest control, a different visit from August’s. What stuck:

  • Soil is fed, not just farmed — chicken manure for nitrogen, cover crops for carbon, black tarp to smother weeds without spraying. The guide’s best line: “Fertiliser is sugar.”
  • Mummified aphids — a parasitoid wasp grows inside a living aphid. A pest that looks dead and is really a nursery, which is the problem statement on a leaf.
  • Buy some, plant for others — the farm buys lacewings and Aphidius wasps, but plants habitat for ladybugs because bought ones fly away.
  • The pest-control ladder — beneficial insects, then row covers, then crop rotation, and only in a bad outbreak a certified organic spray. The lacewing is rung one of four.

Back home, two breeding problems showed up: food (aphids are scarce outside the farm) and cannibalism. The presentation board and the play were both introduced that afternoon. See Biodiversity at the farm.

Farms & expertsRaising insectsSource: Journal + Chat

The expert interview — Tracy, Rincon-Vitova

The first real expert interview of the season, over Zoom at the insectary, and it changed the design. The kids asked eight prepared questions. Three answers mattered most:

  • Ants. “Ants protect the pests because they like the honeydew. They’ll fight off the beneficial insects and eat them.” Releasing lacewings into a garden with an ant trail may do very little.
  • Separate or not — both are right. Her September email said separate the larvae. On Zoom she said put a few eggs per container and let the strongest survive. Different goals: separate to observe, don’t bother to produce — which is the gap the project is built on.
  • An open question. Asked whether lacewings need a wet sponge, as ladybugs do: “I don’t know. That would be good to find out … a good experiment for you guys.”

Design changes that followed: accordion-folded paper instead of cardboard, no water for larvae at all, fresh aphids and fruit flies over moth eggs. The full question-and-answer record is on Lacewing Project.

The same morning Meiling scouted Fox Point Farms looking for aphids to feed the larvae — none in a winter backyard, and the farm was closed on Saturday — and found a visit worth making with the kids. In the evening each child took larvae home: Kyle adopted five and named them (Nice, Yip Yip, Yikes, Fire, Exape); Lindsey has five too.

Planning moved the same night: with no November tournament, 22 November became the target to finish everything, and a video for the sharing booth was suggested.

Feeding experiments begin

The food problem turned into tests. A parent tried mashed boiled yolk as a droplet, and the larva seemed to eat it. Meiling found an artificial diet from a real USDA study (ground beef, liver, egg, brewer’s yeast, honey, sugar, wrapped in stretched Parafilm) and set out a proper test: five larvae on the new food, five on the usual aphids as a control, watched daily.

R!SE H!GH’s coach was “very excited” that the team had visited and planned a return visit where the kids would show their public-speaking skills.

Larvae eating, and the lesson about air holes

Close-ups of a larva eating the meat diet: “This little guy really eats the meat.” Two lessons, written the way a notebook would: the container needs holes — otherwise no air and no water, and the larvae die — and, confirmed again, “Yes, they eat each other.”

The board becomes the memory aid

Meiling answered the memory problem in a way that keeps the play: a Performance Guide that splits the script into five numbered sections, one per child, matching the five sections of the board. Speaking order: Cheryl, Kei, Lola, Lindsey, Kyle.

“Read only the spoken lines. Follow the same order every time … Point, look up, and explain. Your board helps you remember.”

Each child has a two-page mission sheet and was asked to use their own words. They record their voice parts, to be joined into a five-minute video; Ivy and Meiling pick and print photos so the kids can place them on the board together at the next meeting. That is the “board as memory aid, play kept” option from the 1 November write-up.

She also set up a professional interview: Cassandra Fieldson, science communicator, malaria researcher and ecologist, will meet the kids by Zoom on Sunday 15 November at 1 pm (she is away the weekend before).
