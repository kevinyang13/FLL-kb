Beekeeping and Honey — the Hive, the Smoker, the Harvest, and Ants

Summary: How a beekeeper looks after, rescues and harvests from a honeybee hive, how bees turn nectar into honey, and how to keep ants off a hive without poison — as shown on the Fox Point Farms tour.

Sources: raw/source-docs/fox-point-bee-tour-2026-10-25.md and photos taken on the tour, 25 Oct 2026 (the guide's own words; see fox point bee tour for claims to check before using any of it publicly)

Last updated: 2026-10-25

Everything below is the guide's account (source: fox-point-bee-tour-2026-10-25.md) unless marked ours.

The hive

The guide called the standard box hive "probably the most recent innovation in beekeeping" — not much changes in the bee world. He mentioned flow hives as a newer, "cooler" design, but "when it comes down to just good hives, these are the standard". (source: part 7)

Part What it does
Wraparound lid The roof
Inner cover Works like an attic: it helps the bees regulate temperature inside. Has a hole in the middle — see the one-way door below
Pollen patty Pollen is what the larvae are fed. A patty bought from a beekeeping company "around like 100 years" gives a rescued hive extra food to get back to strength
Reservoir A small space for supplemental nectar
Wax foundation A sheet that gives the bees a head start on comb, so a hive gets back to full strength faster. The bees pull the wax off evenly and build outward from where they started (source: part 8)
Brood boxes Two boxes at the bottom where the queen only lays eggs
Queen excluder A screen between the brood boxes and the honey box that keeps the queen below, so the top box holds "pure honey, pollen, nectar"
One-way door Fitted to the inner cover's hole before harvest — see below

Wax. The comb is passed round for the kids to feel: very soft, "but its structural integrity is just unmatched". A frame of it holds roughly 10–12 pounds of honey. Melted down, it makes a candle. (part 7)

A frame of comb, held up for the kids (photo, 25 Oct 2026).

On a frame. Honey is stored along the top and edges — it works as insulation, so the heat the bees make in the middle stays there. The brood (the young) sits in the middle at 90–95°F. (part 10)

Rescue

(source: part 1)

  • Rescued colonies go to the sanctuary yard, are revived to full health and are kept or placed back in urban settings — hotels, restaurants, business campuses — where the beekeeper looks after them for the host.
  • Half of the calls are irrigation boxes: a tiny entrance, easy to guard, in the ground so temperature is steadier.
  • After a rescue: extra food (the pollen patty, nectar) and wax foundations to bring the colony back up.

Smoke

The guide demonstrated the smoker and explained why it works. (source: parts 6, 9)

The apiary. The smoker is the dark can on top of the hive next to the one he is opening; the guide opens a hive wearing a veiled hat and no full suit (photo, 25 Oct 2026).

  • Smoke is like a wildfire warning for a neighbourhood: you'd go home and pack up if you had time. The bees go back into their cells and gorge on honey, getting ready to leave.
  • That changes their priorities: "they're thinking more about packing up their home than they are protecting it". It would take many days of smoke before they actually left.
  • The smoker has a bellows; the lid has a small hole at the bottom, and squeezing the bellows "shoots it right in. Pretty simple."
  • Small hives (like the one shown) are opened without a full suit. Big hives need a full suit — "just more chaos", though not "crazy aggressive".

The cave drawing. The guide said a prehistoric cave painting shows a person with a stick and a big flame and a bee five times the person's size — read as a swarm, not a giant bee — and that it is the first sign of people using smoke to work bees. He couldn't remember the date. Unverified — see fox point bee tour.

How honey is made

(source: part 10)

  1. Foragers bring home nectar, which is 60–70% water.
  2. The bees' goal is to dry it to 15–18%.
  3. Mostly they do it by fanning with their wings — the guide's figure is about 170 beats a second — with thousands of bees at once: "their own HVAC system, their own air conditioning service".
  4. An enzyme in the bees' stomachs also breaks the nectar down, giving it "that honey consistency".

A hive that is full at the top and edges, with fresh comb, has "really good honey for them to have through the winter".

How honey is harvested

(source: part 12)

Step What happens
When When a hive is big enough. The guide's new hive won't make honey this year; with weekly care, a giving farm and good weather, next year
Setup Two brood boxes first. Then the queen excluder, then a honey box on top. A second entrance at the top lets bees bring nectar straight in
Two days before Put the one-way door on the inner cover's hole and close the top entrance. The bees go down and can't come back up
Harvest day Lift off the box — "not bother a single bee" — and take it to the honey kitchen
Spin Shave off the wax cappings, put the frames in a centrifuge that "whips it out"
Strain Once, "to make sure there's no impurities … rocks or sticks"
Bottle And sell at the market. "It comes pretty much straight from the hive."

The farm's hives were due to be harvested "any day now".

Heating honey, and "local" honey

(source: part 15)

  • The guide believes there is a temperature threshold above which honey loses its minerals and pollen and becomes "a neutral sweetener". The temperature itself is in a gap in the transcript.
  • For hot drinks, ordinary store honey (he named Kirkland, Costco's) is fine as a neutral sweetener; honey straight from the hive should not go in something very hot.
  • He said honey from within about 40 miles of where you live helps with sinus problems.

Check these before they go on the board — the second and third are the guide's beliefs, not established facts. See fox point bee tour and outreach event.

Keeping ants off a hive — without poison

(source: part 13)

  • The hive's stand is the only access point for ants. The guide smears Tanglefoot, "a goopy sticky substance", on it so ants can't get across.
  • He calls it "the best non-toxic option".
  • It is not perfect: ants are "really industrious". A weed grew beside the stand; the ants climbed it together until it bent over under their weight and made a bridge over the barrier. "I have a huge respect for ants because they can pull off things like that."

Ours: a beekeeper who defends his bees from ants without poison, and ants that win by teamwork, is the same ant as the one in our aphid scene (see lacewing game v2) — the aphids' bodyguard. It is a good example for non-toxic pest management on the board: lacewing project.
