What an FTC team brings

FTC is the programme above FLL — grades 7–12, bigger robots, real engineering. See first lego split. That means these mentors are strong on exactly the things a rookie team is weakest at:

  • Engineering Design Process done properly — testing, documenting, iterating, and explaining it to a judge
  • Judging — they have been through it many times; FLL and FTC judging share the same DNA
  • Team management — meeting structure, who owns what, keeping five kids moving
  • Mechanical design — attachments, gearing, dispensers. The physics does not care which kit it is built from

What to be upfront about

FTC teams build on different hardware entirely, and most also know FLL through SPIKE Prime and Founders Edition — the 15-mission single-robot game. Ours is Future Edition on CS & AI: five missions, four simultaneous roles, a shared field, no gyro. See bioglow missions and driving base.

So on the first call, say plainly: we are Future Edition, here is our rulebook, here are our four roles. Send bioglow missions ahead of time. Good mentors adapt fast; the risk is only if nobody tells them.

Best first ask: have them review the three Technician delivery designs (launcher → ramp → dispenser) and the no-gyro drift problem. Concrete, mechanical, and exactly what was requested.

Keeping it straight with the judges

The Project rubric scores "guided research influenced direction; multiple relevant sources" and the Engineering Design rubric scores "use of guided missions and building/coding resources." Mentors and experts are exactly that — as long as the kids can say what they learned from whom.

Keep a line per conversation in the notebook: date, who, the one thing that changed because of it. See weekly documentation.

And the rule that outranks the rest still applies: mentors advise, kids build. A mentor who touches the robot is a mentor who has cost the team its own story.
