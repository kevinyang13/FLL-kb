## The Idea

A **keystone species** has a much bigger effect on its ecosystem than its numbers would suggest. Take it away and the whole system rearranges — even though it was never the most common thing there.

The name comes from architecture. A keystone is the wedge at the top of a stone arch: small, not obviously special, and if you pull it out the arch collapses.

### Where the term came from

In the 1960s the ecologist **Robert Paine** did something simple in Pacific tide pools: he removed all the ochre sea stars from one stretch of shore and left a neighbouring stretch alone.

Without the sea star eating them, mussels took over completely and crowded almost everything else out. The area with sea stars stayed full of different species. One predator, not especially numerous, was holding the whole community open.

That experiment is why the phrase exists — and it happened in a **tide pool**, which the team can go stand in at Cabrillo. See idea 8 in [[innovation-project-ideas]].

## Classic Examples

| Species | What it does | What happens without it |
|---------|-------------|-------------------------|
| **Sea otter** | Eats sea urchins | Urchins explode, strip the kelp forest, leave bare rock for decades |
| **Ochre sea star** | Eats mussels in tide pools | Mussels take over; diversity collapses |
| **Beaver** | Dams streams into ponds and wetlands | The wetland dries up, and everything that needed it leaves |
| **Gray wolf** | Preys on elk and deer | Overgrazing strips streamside plants; rivers erode |
| **Prairie dog** | Digs burrow systems | Dozens of other species lose their shelter |
| **Fig trees** | Fruit when little else does | Fruit-eaters starve in the lean season |
| **Bees and pollinators** | Move pollen between plants | Plants fail to set seed; the plant community thins out |

## Southern California

### Coyotes in San Diego's canyons — a world-famous study, done here

In 1999 **Kevin Crooks and Michael Soulé** published a study in *Nature* looking at **37 isolated canyon fragments in urban coastal San Diego County** — patches of chaparral surrounded by houses.

What they found:

1. Smaller canyon fragments supported **fewer coyotes**
2. Where coyotes thinned out, smaller predators increased — foxes, raccoons, and above all **domestic cats**
3. Where those increased, **native scrub-breeding birds disappeared**

The coyote was holding the system together by keeping mid-sized predators in check. Remove it and songbirds vanish from the canyon — not because coyotes helped birds directly, but because coyotes suppressed the things that eat them.

The researchers singled out **house cats** as the strongest single effect, for a reason kids grasp instantly: a wild predator stops hunting when prey gets scarce, because it goes hungry. A pet cat is fed at home, so it keeps hunting anyway.

**This happened in the same canyons the team lives beside.** It connects directly to idea 5 (road crossings and habitat fragmentation) and idea 6 (canyon invasives) in [[innovation-project-ideas]].

### Others worth knowing locally

| Species | Role |
|---------|------|
| **California ground squirrel** | Its abandoned burrows house burrowing owls, snakes and amphibians that cannot dig their own |
| **Sea otter** | The classic kelp protector — but note the southern sea otter's range sits well north of San Diego today, which is part of why urchins go unchecked here |
| **Oak trees** | Feed and shelter hundreds of species; more properly a *foundation* species (see below) |
| **Soil microbes and fungi** | Break matter down and move nutrients to plant roots — the underground version of the same idea |

## Three Terms People Mix Up

Worth getting right, because a judge might ask and precision reads as real understanding.

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|------|---------|---------|
| **Keystone species** | Big effect **relative to how few there are** | Sea otter, ochre sea star, coyote |
| **Foundation species** | **Builds the habitat itself**, and is usually abundant | Kelp, oak trees, coral |
| **Ecosystem engineer** | **Physically reshapes** the environment | Beaver, ground squirrel |

They overlap, and people use "keystone" loosely for all three.

**A correction worth making:** kelp is usually classed as a *foundation* species, not a keystone — it is abundant and it *is* the habitat. The **sea otter** is the keystone in that story. A team that says this correctly sounds like it actually read something.

## How Our Game Models It

The BIOGLOW field is a simplified version of a real ecological argument:

| In the game | The real idea |
|-------------|---------------|
| Keystone species placed into microbiomes (M01) | Add the species back and the system stabilises |
| Keystone species cycled through the waterfall (M03) | Populations need to keep replenishing, not just exist once |
| The invasive queen, knocked down for the bonus (M01) | Invasive species destabilise; removing them helps the natives |
| Level Up: invasive species added, then contained | Prevention is cheaper than cleanup |

Worth telling the kids: **the game is making an argument**, not just handing out points. The high-value action is putting the right species back in the right place — which is what restoration work actually is.

## Using This In Judging

Three sentences worth having ready, in the team's own words:

1. **What it is** — "a species that matters way more than how many of them there are"
2. **A real example** — the sea otter and the kelp, or the coyotes in our own canyons
3. **Why it is in our game** — "M01 gives us 20 points for putting keystone species back, because that is what people actually do to fix an ecosystem"

If the team's project also involves a keystone or foundation species, say so out loud. Connecting the project to the robot game is exactly the kind of thing the Engineering Design rubric's *communicate* rows reward. See [[judging-and-awards]].
